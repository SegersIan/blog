**How do we design for agents and humans to work on the same problems?** Everyone's asking it. Get it right, unlock productivity gains, competitive advantage, glory. 
But here's the challenge: most people asking this question haven't actually figured out what agents *are*, let alone how they change the nature of work. So let's think about it properly.

# The scale of automation operating modes, and how they work together.

## Traditional Automation
Traditional automation executes a workflow of human predefined steps. The focus is on executing the workflow according to the static process (*the how*) with no or little human intervention. The accountability of what the system does, lies with the one who builds and runs it. Any changes to the process (usually) requires another human (an engineer) to modify the system so it follows a new version of that workflow. If the system executes the human predefined steps, the human bears the accountability who defined the process. The responsibility of the workflow might end up with the human that builds and operates it (e.g. An engineer), but the one who defines the process has the accountability if the process/workflow itself is flawed.  It is also not very adaptive to unexpected conditions, or at least, the human must map all unexpected potential conditions and implement this. Often the best option here is to rely on human judgement and reasoning to decide how to tackle unique, unpredicable conditions. Traditional automation is inherintly deterministic. (emphasis on HOW, accountability model, deterministic)

There is little difference between a human and an IT system when it comes to following, clear intstructions, predefined steps. The human has the advantage to be more adaptive for unforseen circumstances, but gets tired and might loose focus. IT systems don't tire or loose focus and might go even faster, but are often less adaptive during unforseen circumstances. In case of social-technical systems (like serving a drink), only humans can bring that true human element (a smile, that human presence, warmth) which might be important to the human who interacts with "the process/workflow".

## Agents
Agents execute with a goal in mind, not a set path of steps. We might even not care about the how, the steps or path taken, as long the desired outcome is reached. The workflow or process is not at the center now, but the outcome. Now there is a human who is responsible for building and operating the agents and their infrastructure, but the actual actions and steps taken are non determinstic, which is required to trade-off for high adaptability to unforseeen or challenge circumstances or input. But who is accountable for the steps taken by an agent? For the good AND the bad.

## according to claude

Accountability is the obligation to answer for your actions, decisions, and their outcomes — to yourself, to others, or to a system. It means you can be asked "why did you do that?" and you're expected to have a defensible answer.

**What purpose it serves**

Accountability exists to close the loop between authority and consequences. When someone has the power to act, accountability ensures that power doesn't drift into negligence or self-interest unchecked. It creates a feedback mechanism: actions produce outcomes, outcomes get evaluated, and that evaluation feeds back into future behavior. Without it, you get entropy — people optimizing for comfort, not results.

It also serves as a coordination mechanism. In any group effort, accountability makes commitments credible. If I say I'll deliver something and there's no accountability, my commitment is just noise. Accountability turns promises into something others can actually plan around.

**Why it matters**

Here's where I'd push you a bit: most people think accountability is about blame. It's not. Blame is retrospective and punitive. Accountability is structural and forward-looking. The point isn't to catch people failing — it's to create conditions where people take ownership *before* things go wrong.

There's a common bias worth flagging: people tend to want accountability for others but autonomy for themselves. "Hold the team accountable" really means "make sure *they* do what *I* expect." True accountability is symmetrical — it applies to you with the same force you'd apply it to anyone else.

Another angle that's often missed: accountability without authority is toxic. If you hold someone accountable for an outcome they had no real power to influence, you're not creating accountability — you're creating learned helplessness. The precondition for accountability is that the person actually had meaningful agency over the thing in question.

Finally, accountability is only as good as the clarity that precedes it. You can't hold someone accountable against a standard that was never articulated. This is why so many accountability failures are actually *clarity* failures in disguise — vague expectations dressed up as someone "not delivering."
